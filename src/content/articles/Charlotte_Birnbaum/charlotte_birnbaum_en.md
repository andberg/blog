Charlotte Birnbaum’s passion for the often-forgotten crafts of culinary art is both inspiring and infectious. Over coffee and coconut macaroons, I hear fascinating stories about sugar, offal, napkin folding, and much more. Each topic has at least one book devoted to it in the “On the Table” series, edited by Birnbaum and designed as miniature works of art by artist Christa Näher.

## “Three Banquets for a Queen”, “The Beauty of the Fold”, “Paulas Juwelen” & “Bon! Bon!”

Charlotte Birnbaum is the editor of Sternberg Press’s “On the Table”, a book series devoted to culinary art, extravagant dinners, spectacular table settings, and related subjects. One of the titles in the series—and the first Charlotte pulls out—is “Three Banquets for a Queen”. The book draws on a historical document rediscovered fifteen years ago, recounting the banquets held in honour of Queen Christina during her visit to Rome. All the menus have survived: hundreds of dishes, many more like sculptures and elaborate showpieces than edible food.

“I want to celebrate the creativity and imagination of pastry chefs. They helped create some of the most spectacular dining experiences. Artists such as Bernini and Leonardo also made sugar sculptures. It was a fashionable material: ephemeral, but appealing.”

**How did you come to make books about art and food?**

“I love eating,” she says, enjoying a coconut macaroon. “That’s where it all begins for me. I find what happens at the dining table so exciting. It’s a new exhibition every day, one you sometimes share with others and sometimes enjoy alone. I studied both art history and theatre studies, and to me the dining table is ‘theatre on the table’. The table is the stage, the food becomes scenery or sculpture, and the diner responds to this theatre.”

Charlotte now brings out “The Beauty of the Fold: A Conversation with Joan Sallas”, describing Joan Sallas as the Carl Linnaeus of napkin folding, a figure entirely in a class of his own.

“He has catalogued all the folds, named them, and documented them in this book. He began his work in a basement, but now he has emerged into the spotlight. A couple of years ago, he exhibited at the Hallwyl Museum in Stockholm.”

## “Pies, Pâtés and Pastries”

**Can you tell me more about your books?** I ask, managing a sip of coffee and a bite of the pastry—which Charlotte has carefully pointed out she did not bake herself—while my host fetches her book “Pies, Pâtés and Pastries: Secrets Old and New of the Art of Cooking”, a work devoted to pies and pâtés.

“Pies and pâtés have been both poor people’s food and a staple of grand occasions. For this book I read about every imaginable kind. Once, in the fifteenth century, an entire orchestra was enclosed inside a pie, alongside a banquet table laden with food.”

“Do you mean a real orchestra, with living people?” I have to ask.

> The pie was opened and out came birds, frogs, and eels. It was not unusual to find scantily clad women or singing boys inside pies, either.

“Yes! It was not at all unusual to put entertainment or conversation starters inside a dish. The pie would be opened and out came birds, frogs, and eels. It was not unusual to find scantily clad women or singing boys inside pies, either. But most commonly there were dwarfs inside. They would emerge to greet the guests with banners, and many royal households had favourite dwarfs who could be borrowed for special banquets.”

“Aristotle wrote about the role of pies during theatrical performances in Athens. They were sold at stalls, rather like the hot-dog stands outside arenas today. Aristotle describes how pies sold particularly well during the duller parts of a play. And there were all sorts of pies. Today we might think mainly of liver pâté, but there are no limits to what can go into this kind of dish. They can be vegetarian, sweet, or filled with fish. I read about an English wife who made her husband a pie bearing his initials, divided into three sections: one was a main course, one a starter, and the third a dessert.”

Charlotte tells me that she has tried all the recipes in her books herself, unless otherwise stated. And she has made them simple.

“I’m only a moderately good cook myself. I prefer sitting in dusty libraries doing research.”

## “The Inner Journey”

**What was your first book?**

“It was the book about offal. I was sitting in the library at Grythyttan, which houses Tore Wretman’s large collection of cookbooks. I couldn’t afford to stay at the elegant inn in Grythyttan, so I rented accommodation at a chicken farm. After I had started working on the book, my family and I moved to Germany. I was determined to carry on writing, but in German. That’s how I learned the language. I didn’t speak German before, and if you read German reviews of ‘The Inner Journey’, several mention that I have a distinctive writing style. But that’s probably just because I was learning as I went along,” Charlotte laughs.

## “Bon! Bon!”

**When did you start collaborating with illustrator Christa Näher?**

“I started working with Christa Näher on my very first book, in 2008. I had seen her art before and loved it. We work very closely together. She lives in southern Germany, but we often send things back and forth. It’s a living collaboration. Sometimes she sends me images that inspire stories and recipes, and sometimes it’s the other way around.”

**Tell me about your latest book.**

“My latest book came out last year, in 2013, and is called ‘Paulas Juwelen: Kulinarische Streifzüge durch das Jahr’ (roughly, ‘Paula’s Jewels: A Culinary Journey Through the Year’). It follows the seasons through their ingredients, and I’ve given each day its own story, recipe, or something else inspired by a historical event, anniversary, death, birthday, or similar occasion. One example is April 3, which features the quotation ‘Essen macht Spaß, viel Essen macht viel Spaß. – Unbekannt’ (‘Eating is fun; eating a lot is a lot of fun. – Unknown’).”

“So far the book is only available in German, but I hope it can be translated soon.”

“‘Paulas Juwelen’ has left a little emptiness behind,” Charlotte says. “Over the years I had collected so many stories that finally found their place in this book. Others I had to seek out deliberately in history books, cookbooks, political writings, fashion, and more. Right now, when the book has only just gone to print, it feels like a separation.”

Charlotte takes out a historical cookbook, and we leaf through it together.

“I find it fascinating, from the perspective of intellectual history, to experience the past through what people ate. Most of what has survived comes from the upper classes. Food was a way of advertising themselves. They held sugar banquets to glorify themselves, but also to conduct politics. If their sugar landscape included a fortress with a particular architecture, it could signal that they intended to attack, for example, Turkey.”

“There is also evidence that the Romans would give their children cakes decorated with letters, and once the children had learned their ABCs, they were allowed to eat the cake as a reward.”

## “Les Dîners de Gala”

**Is there anyone you would like to collaborate with, living or dead?**

“Salvador Dalí! He wrote the fantastic ‘Les Dîners de Gala’ together with the chefs at Maxim’s. It’s full of imaginative banquets. It would be a dream to bring something from it to life.”

**Who inspires you?**

“Christa really inspires me. And I love the Baroque.”

**Which dish would you like to see brought to a wider audience today?**

“The pie! I think sourdough and sausages have had their moment, and now it’s time for the pie. It’s a great way to use leftovers. Personally, I think we need to eat less meat in the West, but when we do eat it, we should use the whole animal. Pies and pâtés can make that fun.”

## “The Futurist Cookbook”

**What are you working on now?**

“I’m going to write the foreword to a new translation of the Italian Futurist Filippo Tommaso Marinetti’s book ‘The Futurist Cookbook’, which will be published in the ‘On the Table’ series. Marinetti was an eccentric character who had a problematic relationship with Mussolini. As an Italian Futurist, he was obsessed with speed, the future, and sweeping away everything old.”

In the cookbook, he creates a new vocabulary for dishes: he wanted to replace French culinary terms with Italian ones.

“And I’d also like to make a version of ‘Paulas Juwelen’, but about drinks.”

As our conversation draws to a close, the large sofa in Charlotte Birnbaum’s home is covered with books, both her own and those of others. Writings about art, food, politics, and love that, together with our conversation, have forever changed the way I see what food can be.

Amid today’s flood of commercial cookbooks and destructive diet culture, books such as “Bon Bon: sockerkonster” and “Den inre resan: en kokbok om matlagning med inälvor” are creative oases where food can exist free of the constraints of our time. An inner journey from which I never want to return.
